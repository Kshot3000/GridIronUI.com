The player props playbook

Props are the most bet — and most misunderstood — market in the book. They price differently, tax differently, and reward different skills than sides and totals. Bet them like a side and the juice eats you; bet them like a prop and the menu gets a lot friendlier.

Why props are a different game

A book posts one spread and one total per game, watched by every oddsmaker on staff and hammered by every sharp in the country. Then it posts two hundred player props for the same game — receiving yards for the third receiver, first-basket scorer, goalie saves — each with a fraction of the attention and a fraction of the betting limits. That asymmetry is the whole story of props:

The opportunity: books can't price 200 numbers as tightly as 2. Their models lean on automation, and automation misses context — a backup thrust into a starting role, a matchup the depth chart explains, a game-script shift. A bettor who actually watches the usage data is competing against a spreadsheet, not a trading desk.

The tax: books know all of this, so they defend themselves three ways — heavier juice (props are routinely −115/−115 instead of −110), lower limits (a book that takes $5,000 on a spread might take $500 on a prop), and fast limits on winners (beat props consistently and your max bet shrinks — getting limited is the compliment nobody wants). Props are beatable and capped: the edge is real, the ceiling is low. Size your expectations accordingly.

The three things that move a prop

Every prop line is downstream of three inputs. Check all three before you bet; most losing prop tickets skipped at least one.

1. Usage. Opportunity is the engine of production. For an NFL receiver: targets, snap share, routes run. For an NBA player: minutes and usage rate. For an MLB batter: lineup spot. A player can't outproduce his opportunities — a receiver running 35 routes at 8 targets a game is a different bet than one running 20 routes, even if their yardage lines look similar. Usage stats are public; use them.

2. Matchup. Defenses have weaknesses by position, not just overall. A cornerback shadow, a linebacker group that bleeds receiving yards to tight ends, a pitcher who can't miss bats — the matchup shapes the distribution of outcomes, not just the average. Pace matters too: more possessions mean more chances for everyone.

3. Game script. The expected shape of the game decides which props are live. A big favorite runs the ball and bleeds clock in the second half (bad for the underdog's passing props, good for the favorite's rushing props); a big underdog throws 45 times playing catch-up. Before betting any prop, ask what the spread implies about how the game will be played — then bet the props that fit the script, not the ones with the famous names.

The median, not the mean

This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in props betting. Books set prop lines near the median outcome — the middle of the distribution — because that's the number that splits the betting public. Bettors handicap with the mean — the season average — because that's the number on the stat page. When a player's production is volatile, those two numbers aren't close, and the gap is where money dies.

Worked example — the average lies. A wide receiver's last five games: 41, 55, 68, 72, and 148 receiving yards. His average is 76.8 yards — the stat page screams "over machine." But the median is 68: half his games land below it, half above. The book posts the line at 68.5. The bettor who saw "averaging 77" and hammered the over paid for one explosion game (the 148) that tells you nothing about a typical Sunday. Always ask: what does this player do on a normal day? — that's the number you're betting against.

The rule: compare the line to your median projection — your honest 50/50 outcome — not to the average. High-variance players (deep threats, bench scorers, home-run hitters) are where the mean-vs-median gap is widest, which is exactly why their props look so tempting and cash so rarely.

The juice math on props

Sides and totals live at −110 both ways: you need to win 52.4% of bets to break even. Props routinely post at −115/−115 — the book's defense tax — and that moves the break-even line to 53.5%. That 1.1-point gap sounds small until you compound it: over 200 prop bets, it's roughly two extra wins you need just to stand still.

Worse, popular-player overs get shaded: the book knows the public loves betting stars to go over, so the over carries extra tax (−125, −130) while the under sits at a friendlier price. The market isn't accusing the star of going under — it's charging the crowd for the privilege of rooting for him. Run every prop through the implied probability calculator and ask what win rate the price demands; then ask whether your edge clears it with room to spare. The EV calculator makes this explicit: a prop you win 55% of the time is +EV at −110 and a slow loser at −130.

Shopping props is worth more

Here's the good news that pays for the juice tax: books disagree on props far more than on sides. A spread might vary by half a point across the market; a receiving-yards prop can vary by two full yards, and the juice on each side varies too. On a 62.5-yard line, one yard is a much bigger slice of the outcome than half a point is on a 47.5 total — so every yard you shop is worth proportionally more.

Worked example — the yard that was the edge. Your median projection says a receiver lands at 64 yards. Book A posts over/under 62.5 (−110 both sides); Book B posts 63.5 (−110). At Book A you're buying 1.5 yards of cushion under your number — a real edge. At Book B you're buying half a yard — basically a coin flip at −110, which is a losing proposition. Same player, same game, same afternoon: one book holds a bet, the other holds a donation. This is why the odds board highlights the best price per outcome — on props, the best number is often the entire edge.

The discipline: never bet the first prop number you see. Check three books minimum. Books move props at different speeds, and a stale number at one book — the kind our board flags when a price sits far off consensus — is the closest thing props have to free money. Your props toolkit on this site

Props reward information speed. This site's live pages are a prop bettor's pregame checklist:

🩹 Injury reports — first, always. Nothing moves props faster than injury news. A starting running back ruled out doesn't just move the spread — it rewrites every backup's rushing, receiving, and touchdown prop, and the books' automated lines are slowest to catch the second-order effects (the backup's backup, the fullback's red-zone role). When news breaks, check the props before the books finish repricing.

🌬️ Game-day weather. Wind is a passing-game tax: sustained winds above ~15 mph suppress passing yards and field-goal props while pushing teams to run. Cold alone matters less than bettors think; precipitation matters for ball security more than yardage. Check the wind flag before touching any quarterback or kicker prop.

📊 Odds board best-price highlighting. The board's consensus line is your anchor: a book hanging a prop a full yard off the consensus is either asleep or knows something. Either way, you want to see it before you bet the consensus number somewhere else.

🧮 The calculators. Implied probability (what win rate does −125 demand?), EV (does your 56% estimate survive the juice?), Kelly (how much is this edge actually worth in units?). Props are small edges with heavy juice — the calculators keep the math honest.

📓 The bet journal. Log every prop with its closing line. Props are where most bettors feel sharp and are average — the journal's record and "beat the close" stat will tell you which one you are within a month.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the shaded star. A star wideout's receiving line opens at 82.5 (−110 both sides) on Monday. By Friday it's 84.5 with the over shaded to −125 — the public hammered the over all week because he's their guy. The move isn't information; it's fandom, and the book is charging for it. The sharp question isn't "will he go over?" — it's "is my median above 84.5 and does it clear a −125 break-even (55.6%)?" Usually the answer is no, and the value — if any — is on the under at the now-friendly price. Never pay the entertainment tax.
Example 2 — the injury ripple. Sunday morning: a team's lead running back is ruled out. The spread moves 1.5 points within minutes — the books are awake. But the backup's rushing line sits at 38.5 for another twenty minutes, and the third-down back's receiving line hasn't moved at all. The first-order number (the spread) repriced instantly; the second-order numbers (the backups' props) lagged. This window is the entire props game: news travels faster than the menu. Check the injury wire early, know the depth chart before the news, and act on the ripple, not the headline.
Example 3 — the wind under. Forecast: 22 mph sustained winds at kickoff for a Sunday game. The total drops 3 points — the market got the memo. But the quarterbacks' passing-yard props drop only half a yard at most books, because prop traders update slower than the totals desk. Wind is a passing tax and a running subsidy: the under on passing yards (and the over on rushing attempts) still holds value after the total has moved. Cross-check the weather page wind flags against the prop menu — when the total moved and the props didn't, the props are stale.
Example 4 — the correlation trap. A same-game parlay: quarterback over 275.5 passing yards and his top receiver over 82.5 receiving yards, priced at +240. Feels like a story — and it is: if the QB throws for 300, the receiver probably ate. The book's parlay pricer knows these outcomes are correlated and has already discounted the payout for it (a fair price on two correlated legs would be far shorter than multiplying the individual odds). Correlated SGPs aren't secret value — they're pre-taxed. The only correlated edges are the ones the pricer missed, and those are rare by design. Size them like lottery tickets, because that's what they are.

The five traps

1. The entertainment tax. Betting your fantasy players' overs, your team's stars, the nationally televised names. The book shades exactly these lines because it knows you'll bet them anyway. If your prop card reads like a Pro Bowl ballot, you're paying retail.

2. Averages instead of medians. The 148-yard explosion game is on the stat page forever and in the player's typical Sunday never. Handicap the median; let the crowd bet the average. (Back to the math ↑)

3. One book, one number. Betting the first prop line you see is volunteering for the worst price on a menu where books disagree by yards. Three books minimum, every time — the shopping is the edge as often as the pick is.

4. Ignoring the juice. A 54% winner at −110 is profit; the same 54% at −125 is a slow bleed. Props live in heavier juice than sides — if you're not converting every price to a required win rate, you're guessing at the only number that matters.

5. The full menu. Two hundred props per game is a buffet designed to make you overeat. Every additional prop is another −115 coin flip against a faster market. Two or three props you handicapped deeply beat twelve you liked the look of — the journal will prove it.

Rules that keep you alive

  • Project the median, bet against the line. Your number is a 50/50 outcome, not a season average. If you can't state your median projection in one sentence, you don't have a bet.
  • Convert every price to a required win rate. −115 needs 53.5%, −125 needs 55.6%, −130 needs 56.5%. The implied probability calculator does it in seconds — no prop gets bet until its price is translated.
  • Shop three books or don't bet. On props, the line you take matters more than the side you like. A yard of shopping beats a unit of handicapping.
  • News first, menu second. Injuries and weather move props before the lines finish moving. Check the injury reports and weather page before opening the prop menu, every slate.
  • Expect to get limited — and take it as a signal. If a book cuts your prop limits, your process is working and your ceiling there is set. Spread action across books, keep records, and never confuse "can't get down" with "should bet bigger elsewhere."
  • Track closing line value on every prop. The journal's "beat the close" stat is brutally honest about props: if you're consistently getting worse numbers than the close, the menu is beating you. One month of logged props tells you the truth.
